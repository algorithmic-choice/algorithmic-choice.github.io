What Does an Algorithmic Choice Demonstrate?

Computational Evidence, Verification and the Limits of Algorithmic Decisions

Nuno Valenzuela Alonso
Independent Scientific Research
Abstract Research Questions Conclusion Scientific Paper

Abstract

What does an algorithmic choice actually demonstrate? This paper introduces a philosophical-computational framework distinguishing an observed algorithmic selection from a demonstrated preference. We establish this distinction through the central logical formula:

Selected h (b) ⊭ DPhC (b, a)

The formula establishes that selecting an action does not, by itself, demonstrate its preference over another alternative. Such a conclusion requires sufficient comparative evidence under explicitly defined standards.

This distinction addresses a fundamental need in artificial intelligence governance, particularly in the European Union, where transparency, traceability, and accountability are central requirements for trustworthy AI.

The contribution provides a formal foundation for determining what an AI system actually demonstrates through its decisions, rather than merely what it selects.

Research Questions

Three fundamental questions clarify the relationship between algorithmic selection, computational evidence and justified conclusions. Select each question to explore its formal and philosophical-computational foundations.

01 What can be established from an observed algorithmic decision?

An observed algorithmic decision establishes which action was selected, but not necessarily why it was selected, whether all relevant alternatives were compared, or whether the selected action is optimal.

The central philosophical-computational distinction is expressed by:

Selected h (b) ⊭ DPhC (b, a)

Selection alone does not logically entail demonstrated preference. A justified preference attribution requires admissible comparative evidence under an explicitly defined standard.

02 Under what conditions does computational evidence support claims of completeness or optimality?

Computational evidence supports completeness when the relevant search space has been fully accounted for, either through exhaustive examination or through formally justified exclusion of alternatives.

Optimality requires stronger evidence: proof that no admissible alternative exceeds the selected action under the declared objective.

For a maximization problem, one sufficient certification condition is:

∀ a∈ A(s) ∖ {b} , L(b) ≥ U(a)

Where A(s) is the complete set of admissible actions, L(b) is a sound lower bound on the selected action's value, and U(a) is a sound upper bound on each alternative's value.

This condition establishes global optimality only when the bounds are valid for the same objective and decision horizon and all admissible alternatives are accounted for.

Exhaustive exploration is not always necessary; sufficient mathematical evidence can establish optimality without examining every possibility individually.

03 How can verification distinguish a successful execution from a justified conclusion?

A successful execution demonstrates that an algorithm completed its procedure and produced an output. A justified conclusion requires an additional evidential assessment.

Verification must establish that the evidence is admissible, that the relevant comparison is resolved, that the evidence corresponds to the actions being compared, and that its direction and scope support the claimed conclusion.

The fundamental inference is:

Valid comparative evidence and satisfied attribution conditions Justified preference attribution

The executable verifier developed in Paper 2 operationalizes this distinction through explicit comparison standards, traceable evidence and computational checks.

The philosophical-computational principle is that successful execution establishes what a machine did; verification establishes what its evidence permits us to conclude.

Central Scientific Conclusion

An algorithmic decision is an observable computational event. Preference, completeness and optimality are distinct claims that require their own standards of evidence and justification.

The contribution of this research is to establish the logical boundaries of these claims and provide a foundation for their computational verification—a distinction particularly relevant to transparency, traceability and accountability in trustworthy artificial intelligence.
