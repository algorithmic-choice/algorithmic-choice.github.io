Abstract

What does empirical evidence demonstrate about algorithmic choice? This paper provides a controlled empirical examination of the distinction between an algorithmic choice, the objective quality of that choice, and the evidence available to justify a comparison between alternatives. Using finite MIN/MAX evaluation structures and archived UCT/PUCT search outcomes, we present explicit counterexamples in which the selected action is not the objectively optimal one.

More importantly, complete information is not necessary to establish that one alternative is strictly better than another. In a finite evaluation instance, only five observations suffice to certify strict superiority across every completion compatible with the available evidence. Removing one critical observation allows two compatible completions to produce opposite rankings.

An archived fixed-seed trajectory also shows that increasing computational budget need not monotonically improve the quality of the selected action along that particular run.

The central finding: an observed selection does not establish optimality; incomplete evidence can nevertheless suffice to prove a strict comparison.

The study is self-contained with respect to its numerical demonstrations. Archived search trajectories are empirical witnesses, not independently re-executed runs.

What is being tested?

The paper separates three claims: the selected action recorded by a finite-budget algorithm, the exact MIN/MAX value of that action in the fully specified synthetic tree, and what partial evidence can establish about two alternatives across every compatible completion.

UCT (Upper Confidence bounds applied to Trees) and PUCT (a prior-guided tree-search rule) return actions under finite search budgets. Their returned actions are compared retrospectively with the exact reference evaluation. The experiments use small synthetic trees and a fixed pseudorandom seed; they are numerical witnesses, not estimates of population error rates.

MAX chooses the larger terminal payoff within each reply; MIN chooses the smallest of the resulting reply values.

First result — Four empirical witnesses

Four archived UCT/PUCT decisions are suboptimal relative to the exact MIN/MAX objective of their respective synthetic trees. Each positive gap is a concrete counterexample to a universal optimality claim that includes the corresponding configured run.

More computation is not always better at each measured budget

In one fixed-seed archived trajectory, the selected MIN/MAX value follows 0.90 → 0.65 → 0.90 as the budget changes from 1 to 2 to 8 simulations. This is a finite non-monotone observation, not a general claim about convergence.

Second result — Five observations can suffice

Consider the optimal-last tree with unobserved terminal values allowed to vary independently within [−1, 1]. Five observed leaves give exact bounds for the comparison of a₅ with a₀.

LE⁺(a₅) = 0.950 > −0.300 = UE⁺(a₀)

Therefore, a₅ is strictly superior to a₀ in every compatible completion, without knowing the remaining terminal values. This is a deterministic certificate, not a statistical confidence interval.

What happens when one observation is removed?

Removing the observed value 0.950 leaves four observations and admits two completions with opposite rankings:

Favorable completion1.00−1.00a₅ > a₀
Adversarial completion−1.00−0.30a₅ < a₀

The reduced evidence does not determine the ordering. The five-observation certificate is minimal by inclusion within its specified evidence set, not globally minimal among all possible certificates.

Scope, validation and limitations

The paper is self-contained with respect to its numerical demonstrations: its tables and MIN/MAX arithmetic can be checked without Python or additional software. However, independently re-executing the historical UCT/PUCT choices requires the original implementations, random-number generator, tie-breaking, priors, action ordering, budget definition and environment. These are not supplied with the article.

What is not claimed: a population error rate, a universal failure of UCT or PUCT, an online certificate available during the historical decisions, external replication, or regulatory certification. The synthetic trees are transparent audit artifacts, not evidence of deployed-system reliability.
